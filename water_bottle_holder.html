Clip on Water Bottle Holder

The idea for this design came from a personal and ongoing issue that I kept running into, accidentally knocking over my water bottle in quiet settings like classrooms or at night, where the noise is both unnecessary and embarrassing. After it happened multiple times, it became clear that this was a small but common problem that could be easily avoided with a simple solution. My initial idea was to design a holder that attached off the side of a table; however, as I thought more about stability, ease of printing, and the need to support larger water bottles, this approach began to feel unreliable. Moving the design onto the tabletop allowed for better balance and overall support while still keeping the bottle secure and out of the way. This made the concept more practical and dependable, as it directly prevents bottles from tipping over in the first place. The sketches shown to the right reflect the different iterations and improvements made throughout this idea-development stage, leading into the more refined design process outlined below.
This project began with a search for a table-mounted water bottle holder on Thingiverse, but none of the available designs fit my needs. I then came across a tripod phone clip (click here to view website) and decided to repurpose it into a table holder. Implementing Systematic Inventive Thinking pattern (Addition), I created a custom structure in Tinkercad, designing a rectangular connector to fit the phone clip, adding perpendicular and parallel support arms, and attaching a hollow cylinder to hold the water bottle. Several design and printing issues arose, including misalignment that caused parts to print midair, instability from poor height placement, a cylinder that was too small to fit standard bottles, and a clip unable to support the weight of the full structure. These challenges required several revisions to the design. Ultimately, the limitations of precision and stability led me to transition the project to Onshape, where I focused on improving dimensions, weight distribution, and overall reliability.
In the Onshape phase of the project, I redesigned the model to improve precision, stability, and scalability. I began by creating a new table holder from scratch, intending to secure it to the table using a screw and bolt mechanism. Although this required multiple attempts, it allowed me to better understand tolerances and fastening challenges in 3D printing. I then redesigned the water bottle holder to be significantly larger so it could accommodate a variety of bottle sizes, while also increasing wall height to prevent tipping, even if the holder leaned against a wall. The connector was designed as a separate component to ensure accurate fitting between the table holder and the bottle holder, with added flexible clip features to allow minor adjustments without reprinting the entire model. The most challenging aspect was the screw and boltdesign, which initially failed due to improper threading geometry. After seeking guidance from the maker lab and identifying the lack of a lead-in on the threads, I redesigned the fastener to improve functionality. Overall, the Onshape redesign addressed the limitations of the earlier model by improving fit, weight distribution, and modularity.

OnShape vs. Tinkercad

Overall, Tinkercad was much easier to use when it came to quickly designing simple components, such as the connector and cylindrical holder, and it allowed me to turn an idea into a model very fast. However, that simplicity also led to complications, especially with alignment and proportions, which resulted in issues like gaps, instability, and parts not sitting flat or standing properly. Onshape, while more challenging to learn at first, allowed for a higher level of precision and control, which helped eliminate these problems by making it easier to define exact dimensions and relationships between parts. This difference became especially clear when refining the design to ensure everything was properly aligned and supported. Based on this experience, I would recommend Tinkercad for quick, straightforward, and less complex designs, while Onshape is better suited for more detailed projects that require accuracy, structural planning, and long-term refinement.

Project Review

This project was challenging, but in a way that made the process engaging rather than frustrating. Working through design limitations and printing errors pushed me to problem-solve and think more critically about how digital designs translate into physical objects. It also gave me hands-on experience with new software, specifically Tinkercad and Onshape, which helped me better understand 3D modeling, precision, and design constraints. One of the most rewarding aspects of the project was seeing the idea evolve over time. What started as a rough sketch turned into a functional design through multiple iterations, redesigns, and improvements. Being able to physically test the design, identify what did not work, and then refine it made the project feel very real and meaningful. Overall, the experience not only strengthened my technical skills but also made the design process feel more approachable and enjoyable.

Tinkercad Indepth Process

To begin the design process, I searched for existing models on platforms such as Thingiverse and the NIH 3D Print Exchange, but none aligned with the idea I had in mind. During this search, I came across a tripod phone holder that used a clip designed to hold a phone. I realized that this clip could potentially be repurposed to grip the edge of a table instead. Because the clip already included a built-in cutout, I decided to design the rest of the structure around that feature rather than creating a completely new attachment mechanism. Using Tinkercad, I started the design by creating a rectangular box that fit precisely into the cutout of the phone clip. This piece served as the main connector between the clip and the rest of the structure. I then attached two additional rectangular boxes to extend the design outward: the first rectangular box was positioned perpendicular to the connector, and the second rectangular box was attached parallel to the table surface. This parallel section acted as the support arm. Next, I attached a cylindrical shape to the end of the parallel arm to function as the water bottle holder. To allow the bottle to fit, I removed the center of the cylinder, creating a hollow space sized for a water bottle.

When I attempted to print this first version, several problems became immediately clear. The most significant issue was that the connector between the rectangular arm and the cylinder was not properly aligned on the build plate. Because the shapes were combined at slightly different heights, part of the model was floating above the print bed, causing the printer to attempt printing in midair. This resulted in a failed print. To address this, I returned to Tinkercad and carefully realigned all components, adjusting their positions so that the entire object sat flush against the build plate before exporting the file again. After fixing the alignment issue, additional problems appeared in later prints. The rectangular arm was positioned too high, which caused the cylinder to sit above the table instead of resting on it. This made the holder unstable and unable to stand properly. Additionally, in order to fit the design within the print limits of the Prusa Mini, I reduced the size of the cylinder. However, this made the holder too small to fit most standard water bottles, limiting its functionality. Another major issue involved the phone clip itself. Although it worked well for holding a phone, it was too small to support the added weight of the water bottle and the cylindrical holder. The connector between the clip and the arm was also too large relative to the clip, which made the entire structure easy to tip over once a bottle was placed inside. These issues rendered the deisgn basically useless.

Because of these repeated printing and stability issues, I decided to transition from Tinkercad to Onshape for the next iteration of the design. Moving into Onshape allowed me to be more precise with measurements and constraints. At this stage, my main goals were to increase the size of the cylinder to support larger water bottles, refine the dimensions of each connecting component, and account for the weight of the bottle and the overall structure to create a more stable and reliable final design.

Onshape Indepth Process

To begin the design process in Onshape, I started by working on the table holder. My original plan was to reuse and modify a past design, but after trying to rearrange and adapt it, I realized it would be more efficient to start from scratch. After watching a box-design tutorial in Onshape, I gained a better understanding of the basic tools and workflow. Using this knowledge, I created the table holder by connecting four rectangular sketches to form the main structure. At the bottom, I added a circular feature intended for a screw and bolt system that would allow the holder to tighten securely onto the table. I used the extrude tool with the remove setting to create the hole for this mechanism. In the same step, I also added a rectangular cutout where a connector piece would later fit, allowing the table holder to attach to the water bottle holder.

Next, I moved on to designing the water bottle holder itself. Since my earlier Tinkercad print turned out too small, my main goal was to make this version as large as possible while still being practical to print. I used the sketch tool to create a circular base and then extruded it to form the cylindrical walls. I removed the center to create space for the water bottle and included an opening for the connector. I also made the walls taller and thicker than before so that even if the bottle leaned against the side, it would not tip over or fall out. This was especially important because smaller bottles inside a larger container could be easily tipped over.

Rather than combining the water bottle holder and connector into one piece, I decided to separate them. This choice was influenced by both printing limitations and functionality. Separating the parts allowed me to maximize the size of the water bottle holder so it could support a wider range of bottle sizes. So I designed the connector, making sure it matched the dimensions of the cutouts in the table and water bottle holder. To avoid the risk of having to reprint large parts if the fit was off, I added thin, clip-like features to the connector. These clips were designed to slightly flex and tighten the connection during assembly, meaning that if adjustments were needed, I would only need to reprint the small clips rather than the entire model. This helped optimize both time and material use.

The most difficult and time-consuming part of the entire process was designing the screw and bolt. Initially, I assumed this would be a solved problem, so I downloaded an STL file of a screw and bolt from an online platform and scaled them equally in PrusaSlicer. Although this seemed correct, the printed bolt only screwed in one or two rotations before stopping. When I tried forcing it, the head of the bolt snapped. To troubleshoot, I went to Brandeis MakerLab office hours for help, where a staff member suggested using models from Printables and helped me scale them more accurately, as well as add supports. Unfortunately, this attempt also failed. While this was frustrating, the experience was still valuable, as I learned more about printer behavior and troubleshooting techniques. At that point, I decided to design my own bolt in Onshape. While following a tutorial, I realized the main issue with my previous attempts: neither bolt had a proper lead-in at the start of the threads. Without this feature, the threads could not engage smoothly, which explained why the bolt stopped after only a few rotations. After adding a lead-in and carefully matching the dimensions to the table holder, the bolt design finally made work. Ultimately, the Onshape redesign resolved the key issues from earlier iterations, allowing for precise, stable, and fully functional components. This final version successfully integrated all parts, including the screw and bolt, completing the overall design.
