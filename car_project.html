Building D2-5 Intelligent Tracking Car DIY

This project was inspired by the concept of a self-driving car. The Tracking Car DIY Kit allows you to build a small robotic car that detects and follows a line on the ground using infrared sensors. In our case, the path was created using tape placed on the floor, which the car was programmed to track. The system works by using a red light source that shines onto the surface below the car and reflects back to a photoresistor. Because white and black surfaces reflect light differently, the sensor is able to detect changes in reflectivity. When the sensor passes over a white surface, more light is reflected back to the photoresistor, allowing the car to continue moving forward. However, when the sensor detects the darker line, less light is reflected, which changes the resistance in the photoresistor. This signal tells the control circuit to adjust the motors by slowing or stopping one side, causing the car to turn and return to the line. By continuously detecting these changes, the robot is able to follow the taped path automatically. To begin the project, my partner Lydia and I first practiced soldering on blank electronic metal boards so we could become more comfortable with the process before working on the actual components. Once we felt we understood the basics, we opened the D2-5 Intelligent Tracking Car DIY Kit and organized our workspace to make sure we had all the necessary parts and tools. We then followed the instructions. in the guidebook, which were very clear and easy to understand. Although the steps themselves were straightforward, our main challenge was soldering since it was new for both of us and required precision.
After we finished assembling the first robot, we noticed that the car was not functioning correctly during testing. Specifically, there was an issue with one of the sensors and the left tire. The left sensor appeared to be overly sensitive and reacted even when it should not have, while the right sensor did not seem to detect the line at all. Because the sensors control the movement of the wheels, this caused the car to behave incorrectly and the left tire stopped moving as expected. To troubleshoot the issue, we first attempted to adjust the sensitivity levels of the sensors by lowering the sensitivity on the left side and increasing it on the right side. Unfortunately, this adjustment did not solve the problem. We then tried reassembling the left tire and checking the motor connection, thinking that the issue might have been related to the wheel assembly, but that also did not work. After trying several adjustments, we decided that the best solution would be to rebuild the robot and solder the components again more carefully. Some possible causes of the issue could have included uneven soldering, weak electrical connections, solder accidentally touching multiple pins, or a loose connection between the sensors and the circuit board. Rebuilding the robot allowed us to correct these potential issues and improve the overall quality of the assembly.
Building the second robot was much quicker since it was our second iteration and we had a better understanding of the process. During this build, we paid closer attention to our soldering to ensure cleaner and more secure connections. We also double-checked each component before moving on to the next step so we would not repeat the same mistakes. After completing the assembly, we carefully adjusted the sensor sensitivity levels to ensure that both sensors detected the line correctly and that the wheels responded properly. This second attempt worked much better and allowed the car to follow the line more consistently.
While working on the robot, we also used Onshape to design a cover so the robot would look more like a car. To start, we took measurements of the robot, including its length, width, and height, so the design would fit correctly. Designing the 3D model was relatively straightforward because I already had experience using Onshape from previous projects. It mainly involved adjusting and estimating dimensions to ensure the cover would fit properly over the robot. My partner also contributed by adding additional features to make the design look more like a car. The more challenging part was creating the laser-cut design. Watching this tutorial helped me better understand how to work with the design process in LightBurn. After finishing the basic layout, I added details such as windows, simple headlights using LEDs in the corners, and a license plate number. To prepare the design for laser cutting, I converted the 3D model into a 2D drawing in Onshape and then exported the file into LightBurn, which works similarly to how PrusaSlicer prepares files for 3D printing but is used for laser cutting. Since it was our first time using LightBurn, our initial laser-cut print came out larger than expected, while our 3D-printed cover ended up slightly too small due to measurement miscalculations. After reviewing the dimensions, we adjusted the scale by increasing the size of the 3D print and decreasing the size of the laser-cut design. Once these adjustments were made, the pieces fit together much better and the final result worked as intended.

Project Rating

Overall, this project was a valuable learning experience because it combined electronics, mechanical assembly, and digital design. It helped me develop new skills such as soldering, troubleshooting hardware issues, and designing parts using both 3D modeling and laser cutting software. Although we faced challenges during the first build, rebuilding the robot allowed us to better understand how the components work together and how small mistakes can affect the final result. In the end, the project showed how iteration, problem-solving, and teamwork are important parts of the engineering and design process.
